Immuno­therapy

Giving the body's own defenses the strength to fight cancer.

FIG. 00 / vital signs
Summary by the project author
In plain wordsImmunotherapy is when doctors enhance the human immune system by giving drugs that strengthen it so it can kill the cancer.

How it
works

The immune system is the body's defense. It is made of white blood cells plus the organs and tissues of the lymph system. It spots abnormal cells and destroys them, and it likely prevents or slows many cancers. Immune cells found in and around a tumor are called tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). They show the body is responding, and people whose tumors contain them often do better. Cancer does not give up easily, though. It fights back with three tactics.

Tactic 1

Cancer hides. Genetic changes make cancer cells harder for the immune system to see.

Tactic 2

Cancer shuts the immune system off. Proteins on the cancer cell surface switch immune cells off.

Tactic 3

Cancer corrupts its surroundings. It changes the normal cells around the tumor so they interfere with the immune response.

Source: National Cancer Institute

Five types

Type A

Immune checkpoint inhibitors

A checkpoint is a brake. The immune system uses checkpoints so its response never gets too strong. These drugs block the brake. Immune cells can then hit the cancer harder.

Type B

T-cell transfer therapy

Also called adoptive cell therapy. Doctors take immune cells from the tumor and pick the ones most active against the cancer. They are modified in the lab, grown in large batches, and given back through a vein.

Type C

Monoclonal antibodies

These are lab-made immune proteins built to latch onto specific targets on cancer cells. Some of them mark the cancer cells, so the immune system can see and destroy them.

Type D

Treatment vaccines

These vaccines do not prevent disease. They boost the immune system's response to cancer cells that are already there.

Type E

Immune system modulators

These strengthen the body's immune response against cancer. Some target specific parts of the immune system, and others work more generally.

Source: National Cancer Institute

How it's given

Intravenous

Straight into a vein.

Oral

Pills or capsules you swallow.

Topical

A cream rubbed on the skin. It is used for very early skin cancer.

Intravesical

Delivered directly into the bladder.

Where: a doctor's office, clinic or hospital outpatient unit. There is no overnight stay. How often: every day, week or month. It depends on the cancer type and stage, the immunotherapy, and how the body reacts. Some treatments come in cycles, a treatment period and then a rest period. The rest lets the body recover, respond and build new healthy cells.

Source: National Cancer Institute

Side effects and monitoring

  • Side effects happen because a revved-up immune system also attacks healthy cells and tissues.
  • Doctors do not guess. They track progress with frequent visits, physical exams, questions about how you feel, blood tests and scans that measure the tumor.
  • Side effects depend more on the type of immunotherapy than on the cancer type. If you notice side effects or unusual symptoms, tell your health care team right away.
Sources: NCI and Cancer Research Institute
